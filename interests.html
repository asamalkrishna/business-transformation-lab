Marketing management · consumer behaviour
Mere exposure

Familiarity can create preference.

The more familiar people become with something, the more comfortable or positive they can feel towards it — one reason repeated brand exposure matters.

Anchoring

The first number can change the next one.

An initial reference point can influence later judgements. In marketing, the first price or comparison point can shape what feels expensive or cheap.

Social proof

Other people's choices become signals.

Ratings, reviews, popularity and what others appear to choose can reduce uncertainty — especially when we do not know enough ourselves.

Loss aversion

Losing can feel worse than winning feels good.

People often weigh losses more heavily than equivalent gains. That helps explain why framing and perceived risk matter so much in decisions.

Choice overload

More options are not always better.

A larger menu can sound attractive, but too many choices can increase decision effort and make it harder to choose at all.

Framing

The same fact can feel different.

How information is presented can influence how people interpret it, even when the underlying facts have not changed.

“The interesting question is often not what people chose — but why that choice felt like the obvious one.”

A recurring thought from marketing and consumer behaviour
